== Diagrams are communication

Diagrams are older than writing.
People drew maps, tallies and kinship long before they wrote sentences, and they still reach for a whiteboard when a conversation gets complicated.
You might expect such an old form of communication to be well understood.
In my experience it is not: there is a gap in how people approach diagramming, and it costs organizations a great deal of time.

Like writing, diagramming is not an innate skill.
Being able to connect two boxes with an arrow does not make someone a good diagrammer, any more than being able to write a sentence makes someone a good writer.
And I've seen many diagrams where people can't even connect two boxes correctly - they draw arrows between boxes, but the arrows do not connect to the boxes - so there is no relationship.
This part of the book is about thinking in diagrams: what a diagram is for, who it is for, and how much structure it should carry.

=== What a diagram is

A diagram is a symbolic representation of information, and in the sense this book uses, a particular genre of it: shapes connected by lines, arrows or other visual links, which show things and their relationships.
I extend the genre to quantitative information too, because the size, position and color of a shape can carry numbers as well as categories (<<problem-domain>>).

Diagrams fall into two broad groups.

*Representations only.*
The diagram leaves it to the beholder to work out what a shape means.
A blue rounded rectangle might be a system, a team or a deployment unit, and only a legend, a convention or a conversation says which.
Draw.io, Visio and PowerPoint produce diagrams of this kind.

*Representations in a domain language.*
UML, BPMN, ArchiMate and SysML define what each shape means, so a reader who knows the language reads the diagram without help.

Enterprise IT is dominated by the first kind, and Draw.io and PowerPoint are its common formats.
UML has been around for about thirty years and is still used narrowly and often misused.
Simon Brown, the author of the C4 model, made the same observation: many teams have abandoned the modeling languages for simpler boxes-and-lines diagrams, and in doing so have lost some of their ability to communicate visually.

I think the reason is that every enterprise has a language of its own, rarely written down.
It speaks of products, channels, platforms, capabilities, controls and owners, with meanings that are local to it.
It does not speak UML or BPMN, and a tool that imposes either one is resisted.
Tools that make it easy to build a specialized diagram editor for such a language, Eclipse Sirius among them, are not widely used either, because a specialized editor is one more tool to install, learn and keep.

What remains is the general-purpose editor and the boxes-and-lines diagram, which everybody can draw and nobody can check.

=== Who a diagram is for

A diagram is a message, and a message has readers.
I find it useful to name four of them.

*Yourself, now.*
You draw to understand the problem in front of you and to break it into pieces.
Such a diagram may never be shown to anyone, and that is fine.

*Yourself, later.*
The faintest ink is better than the strongest memory.
What is obvious today will not be obvious in a year, when you come back to a decision and need to know why it was made.

*Other people.*
You draw to explain an intent, to gather feedback, or to instruct.
This is where most diagrams are aimed, and where most of their cost lies: every time a reader has to ask the author what a shape means, the diagram has failed a little.

*Computers.*
You draw to tell a program what to do: to configure it, to generate code or documentation from the diagram, or to execute it.
And, increasingly, *AI assistants*, which read diagrams as context, summarize them for people, or let people ask questions of them.
An assistant is a reader that does not get tired of asking what a shape means, as long as the diagram has an answer.

=== The cost of a diagram

A diagram has two costs.
The *production cost* is the effort of drawing it and keeping it current.
The *consumption cost* is the effort its readers spend to get what they need from it: to find it, to understand it, to ask about what they do not understand, and to act on it.

I call a diagram *efficient* when it is cheap to produce and *effective* when it is cheap to consume.
The two pull against each other.
A sketch is cheap to draw and costly to read, because everything not on the page has to be explained in person.
A diagram with documented elements, links between levels of detail and properties that programs can check is more work to draw and much less work to read.

The right balance depends on the readers.
A whiteboard sketch for a one-hour meeting should stay a sketch.
An architecture diagram that a hundred people consult over three years deserves documentation, and one that drives a deployment deserves properties that a program can check.
The goal is the least *total* effort to communicate, production and consumption together.

=== What I wanted from a diagram

For years I wanted a way of working with diagrams that would let me:

* create a diagram in an editor that everyone can use;
* share it with people who do not have that editor, in a browser;
* document its elements with rich text, beyond what a tooltip holds;
* give its elements sub-diagrams, nested as deeply as needed;
* read it from a program, as data;
* associate behavior with its elements, so that the diagram is executable;
* and map its elements to a model with a defined meaning, when a defined meaning is what the readers need.

Each of these is a point on a continuum, from a bare picture to a model.
