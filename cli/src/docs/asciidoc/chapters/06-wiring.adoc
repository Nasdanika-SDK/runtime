`Adapters.isAdaptable` answers from declarations: an object's own generic supertypes, and the generic types of its `@Adapter` fields and methods, matched with Java's rules for assignment.
The runtime's `common` module implements it, and its Zephyr Book, _Matching and Wiring_, describes the rules in detail.

=== What a command needs from its parent

`@Parent` on a field, or on a method taking one parameter, says what a command needs from its parent: the member's generic type.

* *Matching*, when the tree is built: the command goes under a parent whose class is adaptable to the type of any of its `@Parent` members.
* *Injection*, after parsing and before execution: the parent is adapted to the members' types, the most specific first, and the first value obtained is injected. A command has one parent, so several `@Parent` members are alternative ways to take it, and only one is set.

The runtime's `save` command takes its parent two ways:

=== What a command accepts as sub-commands

`@SubCommand` on a field, or on a method taking one parameter, says what a command accepts as sub-commands.
A member typed as an array, a `List`, a `Collection`, an `Iterable` or a `Set` takes all the sub-commands adaptable to its element type; any other member takes the first.

When the tree is built, a sub-command goes under a command that accepts its class through a `@SubCommand` member, as well as under a parent its own `@Parent` members take.
The two are the two directions of one relation: a child that knows what it needs, and a parent that knows what it accepts.

Sub-commands not on the command line have not had their options parsed when they are injected.
They are there to be inspected, listed or delegated to, as the export command lists its formats.

=== Offers and hand-downs

What a parent offers with `@Adapter`, it offers to every command that might match: any command that needs that type goes under it.
Sometimes a parent has something only for children written for it.
A plain public method, not an `@Adapter`, is an offer only to children that take the parent by its class, `@Parent ExportCommand export`, and call the method.

Offer with `@Adapter` what any child may use; hand down with plain methods what only children written for this parent should use.

=== When wiring happens

`WiringExecutionStrategy` injects the `@Parent` and `@SubCommand` members of the commands on the command line, and the `@Parent` members of their mix-ins, and then executes as its delegate does, picocli's `RunLast` by default.

* *After parsing*: a parent's options are parsed by then, and its adapters see their values.
* *From the root to the executed command*: a parent's own members are in place before it is adapted for its child, so a value can flow down several levels.
* *Not for help or version requests.*

The factories set the strategy on the root commands they create.
A command line assembled by hand needs `commandLine.setExecutionStrategy(new WiringExecutionStrategy())`, as the tests of this chapter do.

=== Rules

* Members are found once per class, on the class and its superclasses, and must be accessible: in a package opened to the runtime, as picocli needs anyway. An inaccessible member fails with a message that names the package to open.
* A `@Parent` or `@SubCommand` member is a field or an instance method with one parameter, never static.
* `@Parent` is unrelated to picocli's `@ParentCommand`, which keeps working as picocli defines it.
