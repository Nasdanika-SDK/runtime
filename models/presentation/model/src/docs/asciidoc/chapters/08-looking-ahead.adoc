== Looking ahead

Everything in this chapter is [.planned]#PLANNED#.

=== Loaders and generators

Each format is a loader and a generator over this model, not a model with ambitions of its own:

* *Draw.io*, through the runtime's Draw.io model, which already reads and writes Draw.io documents: a page per slide.
* *PowerPoint and Visio*, on models of their schemas, PresentationML and DrawingML, and VSDX, rather than on a library of the format; postponed on 2026-10-25.
* *PlantUML*, as an input: diagrams written as text, arriving as editable shapes.
* *PDF*, for the archive, and *web pages*, for sites, generated with the runtime's HTML and SVG models.

=== The notes convention

A YAML or JSON block in the notes read on load into attributes and what a slide depicts, and written back on save (<<a-slide-is-a-diagram>>).

=== Applications

*Visio to Draw.io.*
Enterprises hold large estates of Visio drawings: networks, processes, floor plans, flows of data, maps of integration, accumulated over decades and locked behind licenses that most of the people who need to read them do not have.
Through this model the migration is a batch job: load the drawings, keep shape data as attributes and what shapes depict, emit Draw.io, review the result as a comparison of models, and publish the estate as a site that can be searched.
What arrives on the other side is the first time the estate can be queried.

*One generator, many renderings.*
A quarterly architecture review generated from a model of the architecture comes out as PowerPoint for the meeting, PDF for the record, Draw.io for the wiki, and a page for the portal: one model, four renderings, and no divergence between them.

*Mining the estate of decks.*
The decks an organization already has document its systems, programs, vendors and intentions.
Load them, extract shapes, text and connectors, and mine them into models of the architecture or the work.
Even the shallow result is useful: what is mentioned, how often, by whom, and where the descriptions disagree.

*Presentations as documentation sites.*
Every slide a page, every shape an anchor, notes as the content of the page, connectors as links, and markers pointing back to the file.

*One deck, many audiences.*
The board version, the engineering version, the client version, the version without the numbers: audience becomes a decision of rendering over one model.

*Review, comparison and merge.*
A comparison of models answers "what changed in this deck" with added slides and connections that point elsewhere, which makes a deck reviewable in a pull request.
A merge of three versions, the previous generation as the base, the fresh generation and the deck a person edited, keeps a generated deck alive past its second run.

*Layout.*
With bounds, ports, waypoints and topology in a neutral model, ELK lays out the contents of a slide, as it lays out diagrams, which is what makes a generated deck readable instead of a grid of boxes.
