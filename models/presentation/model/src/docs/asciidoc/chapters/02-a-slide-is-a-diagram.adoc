== A slide is a diagram

Admitting that a slide is a diagram is the entire design.
Everything a deck does that a picture cannot, connect two boxes, nest a group in a group, attach an arrow to a shape, say which element of an architecture a box stands for, the runtime's diagram model already does.

So the presentation model does not invent a parallel hierarchy:

* `Presentation` and `Slide` extend the diagram model's `Diagram`;
* `Shape` extends `Node`, with bounds and ports;
* `ConnectorShape` extends `Connection`, with a source, a target and waypoints.

Whatever works on diagrams works on slide 7 of a quarterly review without a line of code specific to presentations: what elements depict, conversion, layout, comparison, and the model tooling of the runtime.
The presentation model adds only what a deck has and a diagram does not.

=== What a deck adds

*Geometry of the deck.*
A `Presentation` has a `width` and a `height` in points.
Points, not the units of one format or pixels, because a neutral space of coordinates is the condition for a round trip that does not scramble the layout.

*Text as structure.*
A `TextShape` contains paragraphs, and a paragraph contains runs of text with their font and their link.
The words of a slide are a tree that can be queried, not a string with markup in it.

*Masters, which are slides.*
`MasterSlide` extends `Slide`, so a master is walked, queried, mapped and generated by the same code as a slide.
Rebranding an estate of decks is then a transformation over masters, not a person opening files, and a corporate template is a model that a generator targets, not a file it imitates.

*Notes.*
`Notes` are the one thing every format of presentations has and no format of diagrams does.

=== Notes carry annotations

Speaker notes are also the most useful carrier of annotations in the family [.planned]#PLANNED#.
By convention, a YAML or JSON block in the notes is read on load into the slide's attributes and what it depicts, and written back on save.
Every format has a field for notes, it is invisible when presenting, and it survives a round trip through tools that know nothing about the model.
A deck can be annotated in PowerPoint by someone who has never heard of EMF, and come back as a mapped model.

=== What a typed model gives

*What a shape depicts.*
`semanticElements`, from the diagram model, lets a box on a strategy deck point at the element of an architecture model it depicts.
"Which slides mention this system" becomes a query, not a search of a file share.
The link is optional: a shape that depicts nothing is still a documented, addressable element.

*Text as a tree.*
Extract every bullet of a deck, translate a presentation by walking its runs and writing them back, change the font of an estate, feed the text into a search index, or check that no slide uses a term the legal team retired.

*Connectors that are connections.*
A connector has a source and a target, so a slide has topology.
That is what makes a deck convertible to a notation like PlantUML or Mermaid at all: without topology, a slide is a bag of boxes near each other.

*The mechanics of a round trip.*
`order` for the order of drawing, `rotation`, `flipHorizontal` and `flipVertical`, rows and cells with `columnSpan` and `rowSpan`: the unglamorous attributes that decide whether a converted deck opens correctly or scrambled.
`hidden` on a slide is kept because it carries intent: the backup slides, the appendix, the version for the other audience.

*Extension without changing the model.*
The diagram model's `attributes`, from the runtime's core model, absorb what a format carries and the model does not: an index of a PowerPoint placeholder, a row of Visio shape data, a Draw.io style.
Supporting a new format is the work of a loader and a generator, not a release of the model.

*Provenance.*
Every element has markers, so a shape traces back to the file, page, branch and commit it came from, which makes a migration of thousands of files reviewable.
